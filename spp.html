The problem & my responsibility

Connect the work.
Keep the context.

Coaches need to plan, understand athlete context, and review what happened. Athletes need to know what is assigned, carry it out, and see their progress. SPP’s product goal is to connect those responsibilities through a shared view of training and development.

My work spans product direction, the Coach Portal, the Player experience, backend APIs, data modelling, and permissions. I’m responsible for connecting those parts to the workflows they serve.

The platform is in development. Public access and launch timing have not been announced. This case study focuses on selected decisions and published previews.

Decision 01 · Training history

A training plan can change. An athlete’s history shouldn’t.

A coach needs to revise future programming without changing the record of an athlete’s completed session. If the plan and the result lose their distinct meanings, reviewing past work becomes less reliable.

The choice: separate prescription from performance.

SPP stores prescribed exercise data separately from performed results. The public engineering account describes completed results as historical records that remain separate from later programming changes.

Prescribed

Performed

What happened

Completed results retain their historical meaning.

The reason is practical: a change to what comes next should not silently redefine what already happened. This distinction makes the responsibility of each record explicit and gives the product a clear rule for preserving training history.

This is a documented implementation decision. The published account does not include an executable test or a before-and-after record that independently verifies the behaviour. The comparison above explains the model; it is not a test result.

Decision 02 · Organisation & permissions

The right context starts with the right access.

SPP supports organisations containing teams, coaches, and athletes. An athlete completing assigned work, a coach managing a team, and organisation staff each need a different scope of access.

The choice: enforce authority behind the interface.

The public engineering account places authorization in the backend, with explicit organisation and role context. It also distinguishes organisation-level responsibilities from staff working within assigned teams.

The reason is straightforward: access should not depend only on what a screen displays or hides. The practical consequence is that permissions belong in API authorization, data queries, and application workflows as well as the interface.

The public material describes that enforcement; it does not publish cross-organisation or role-scope test results. These descriptions are not a security certification.

Decision 03 · Shared systems

Shared meaning.
Different ways of working.

Coaches need space to plan, review, and coordinate across athletes. Athletes need a focused mobile experience for readiness, assigned training, session execution, and progress.

The choice: share the domain, adapt the experience.

The Coach Portal and Player Portal use a shared backend and domain model, with interaction patterns designed for their role, context, and device. The common foundation connects the concepts; each interface follows the task.

Coach Portal

Planning & review

React and TypeScript for desktop and tablet workflows.

Player experience

Daily execution

React Native and Expo for athlete workflows on mobile.

Shared NestJS APIs and Prisma/MySQL data models provide the underlying system. This lets the product use consistent concepts without requiring coaches and athletes to work through an identical interface.

The architecture and intended workflows are documented. The published Coach previews do not independently demonstrate native-device behaviour or a complete coach-to-athlete sequence.
